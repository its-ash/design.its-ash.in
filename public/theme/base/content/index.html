The Death of the Homepage

Entry points are fragmenting. Readers arrive sideways — from search, feeds, and links — and never see the front door we spent months polishing.

By A. Rivera Senior Editor 14 min read Featured

For twenty years we treated the homepage as the building's lobby: marble floors, a directory, a receptionist. Analytics tell a ruder story. On this very publication, fewer than one visit in five begins at the front door. The rest climb in through windows — a search result deep-links into section three of a reference page, a group chat drops someone onto a two-year-old essay, a feed serves the pull-quote without the argument.

The instinct is to fight for the lobby: bigger heroes, louder carousels, more curation. The evidence says to do the opposite. Every page must now carry the context the homepage used to provide — who we are, where the reader stands, and where to go next.

Wayfinding beats decoration

The publications adapting fastest share a trait: they invest in the furniture of orientation. Persistent section labels. Bylines that link to a body of work rather than a bio modal. Dates that admit their age. "Related" lists chosen by editors, not engagement models.

"A reader who lands mid-archive is not lost. They are exactly where the internet put them. The question is whether your page behaves like a destination or a dead end."

None of this requires abandoning the front page. It requires demoting it — from monument to map. The homepage becomes one view of the archive among many, tuned for the loyal minority who still type the URL.

What we are changing

Starting with this issue, every article carries its section, series, and a hand-picked reading trail in the footer. The archive gains a chronology view. And the homepage, this quaint lobby, will keep its marble — we simply stopped pretending everyone walks through it.
